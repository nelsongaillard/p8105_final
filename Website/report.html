Riding Out the Storm: MTA Ridership through the COVID-19 Pandemic and Weather Variability in NYC

.

December 6, 2025

Jefferson Remo (jr4550), Jonathan Huynh (jkh2157), Nelson Gaillard (ng3005), Mahitha Jangeti (mj3229), Matariya Rattanapan (mkr2158)

Motivation and Related Work

Keith Gessen in The New Yorker writes “[The subway] is considered a national treasure for its diversity, and also very fast if you catch the express from Queensboro Plaza to Sixty-first Street-Woodside”. The Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA) is an essential backbone of New York City, moving millions of riders daily, enabling social, cultural, and economic activity–even for those commuting from their homes to the Columbia University Irving Medical Center.

But the COVID-19 pandemic altered patterns of public transit use, especially in New York, exposing the sensitivity of urban mobility to both public-health and environmental conditions. As proponents of the MTA ourselves, we were interested in learning more about how New Yorkers who rely on buses, subways, and additional forms of public transportation adapted their behavior in response to COVID-19 and varying weather patterns from before the pandemic (2018) to now (2025). Examining how MTA ridership fluctuated with changing case counts and weather conditions can help identify which factors most strongly affected transit demand, ridership behavior, and public transit recovery patterns that carried such modes of transportation to the numbers we see today. These insights can guide policy around public health preparedness, climate variability, and transit safety to help support a resilient transit system that continues to meet the needs of all New Yorkers.

Initial Questions

In this project, we are interested in how MTA patterns changed in response to the COVID-19 pandemic and variability in weather patterns across New York City from 2018 to 2025.

More specifically, we are interested in examining whether increases in COVID-19 cases have led to remarkable reductions in ridership, and whether the magnitude of the responses differed for buses, subways, and additional public transportation services. In addition, in looking at ridership recovery during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, we can analyze how recovery trajectories varied across transit modes. We are also eager to investigate how extreme weather events–such as heavy rainfall, snowstorms, and heat waves–have affected daily public transportation ridership and whether some transit modes remain more resilient to weather changes than others.

Together, these questions aim to clarify how COVID-19 and weather have disrupted urban mobility and what they reveal about the vulnerabilities of public transportation in New York City.

Weather Analysis

Weather data was pulled from ncei.noaa.gov, a site consisting of open data sources regarding weather patterns across the United States. Since weather is measured from multiple sites throughout NYC, Central Park was used as a proxy for all of NYC.

Reading in weather data

nyc_weather =
read.csv("data/nyc_weather.csv")

Cleaning up weather data

weather_df =
  nyc_weather |> 
  janitor::clean_names() |> 
  mutate(
    date = as.Date(date),
    year = lubridate::year(date),
    month = lubridate::month(date),
    month = case_match(month,
                       1 ~ "January",
                       2 ~ "February",
                       3 ~ "March", 
                       4 ~ "April",
                       5 ~ "May",
                       6 ~ "June",
                       7 ~ "July",
                       8 ~ "August",
                       9 ~ "September", 
                       10 ~ "October",
                       11 ~ "November",
                       12 ~ "December"
    ),
    tmin_c = tmin / 10,
    tmax_c = tmax / 10,
    average_temp = (tmin_c + tmax_c) / 2,
  ) |> 
  filter(year %in% c(2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025))

After weather data was loaded, the date variable was coded as a date, and the year and month were extracted such that the month name replaced the month number. This process makes faceting and labeling easier when developing plots. Additionally, originally, the data was stored in the data set as tenths of degrees Celsius (e.g. 250 -> 25°C), so tmin_c and tmax_c were created to showcase the accurate temperature in degrees Celsius. The average temperature (average_temp) was calculated by taking the mean of tmin_c and tmax_c. Lastly, the data was restricted to include only years of interest: 2018 to 2025.

MTA Analysis

MTA data was pulled from data.gov, a site consisting of open data from the US government. Both daily and monthly MTA data was imported.

Read in MTA data

daily_mta = 
  read.csv("data/mta.csv")

monthly_mta =
  read.csv("data/monthly_mta.csv")

Clean monthly and daily MTA datasets & join

monthly_pre =
  monthly_mta |>
  janitor::clean_names() |>
  mutate(
    date = as.Date(month),
    year = year(date),
    month_num = month(date),
    month_name = month(date, label = TRUE, abbr = FALSE),
    ridership = parse_number(ridership),

    mode = case_when(
      agency %in% c("NYCT Bus", "MTA Bus", "Bus") ~ "Bus",
      agency %in% c("Subway","MNR","LIRR","AAR","SIR") ~ agency,
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    ),

    days_in_month = days_in_month(date),
    avg_daily_ridership = ridership / days_in_month
  ) |>
  filter(
    year >= 2018,
    year <= 2019,
    !is.na(mode)
  ) |>
  select(year, month_num, month_name, mode, avg_daily_ridership)

daily_mta_clean =
  daily_mta |>
  janitor::clean_names() |>
  mutate(
    date = mdy(date),
    year = year(date),
    month_num = month(date),
    month_name = month(date, label = TRUE, abbr = FALSE),
    count = parse_number(count),

    mode = case_when(
      mode %in% c("NYCT Bus", "MTA Bus", "Bus") ~ "Bus",
      mode %in% c("AAR","Subway","LIRR","MNR","SIR") ~ mode,
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    )
  ) |>
  filter(year >= 2020, !is.na(mode)) |> 
  mutate(
    weekday = wday(date, label = TRUE),
    weekend = ifelse(weekday %in% c("Sat", "Sun"), "Weekend", "Weekday")
  )

monthly_post =
  daily_mta_clean |>
  group_by(year, month_num, month_name, mode) |>
  summarise(
    avg_daily_ridership = mean(count, na.rm = TRUE),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

full_mta =
  bind_rows(monthly_pre, monthly_post) |>
  arrange(year, month_num, mode) |>
  mutate(
    date = as.Date(paste(year, month_num, "01", sep = "-")),
    period = case_when(
      year < 2020 ~ "Pre-Pandemic",
      year == 2020 & month_num <= 2 ~ "Pre-Pandemic",
      year == 2020 & month_num >= 3 ~ "Pandemic",
      year == 2021 ~ "Pandemic",
      year >= 2022 ~ "Post-Pandemic"),
    period = factor(period, levels = c("Pre-Pandemic", "Pandemic", "Post-Pandemic"))
  )

To clean the data, monthly_mta was divided into data before the COVID-19 pandemic (monthly_pre) and data during/after the COVID-19 pandemic (monthly_post). Like for COVID-19 data and Weather data, year and month were both extracted. Additionally, transit modes were standardized (i.e. combining various bus agencies into Bus). An average_daily_ridership variable was created by taking monthly ridership data and divided it by the number of days in each respective month. For monthly_pre, only observations from years 2018 and 2019 were kept.

The daily_mta dataset was cleaned in a similar way by converting dates (extracting year and month), parsing ridership counts, and standardizing transit modes to be consistent with monthly_pre. Weekend indicators were also added. Cleaned daily data was then aggregated to monthly_post by averaging daily ridership data across each month.

Finally, pre-pandemic and post-2020 monthly datasets are combined in full_mta, sorted chronologically, assigned a standardized date (beginning on the first of each month), and organized into three periods–pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic–based on year and month.

A linear regression model was used to examine whether monthly average temperatures and monthly COVID-19 case counts predicted ridership counts across bus, subway, and “other” transit modes.

For buses, ridership decreases by 6,980 riders for every single unit increase in °C (p = 0.172). Ridership decreases by 1.5 riders for every new case of COVID-19 (p = 0.009). Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic created unprecedented disruptions in the use of public transportation in New York City. Our analysis illustrates how these disruptions altered mobility across the city.

In 2020, at the onset of the pandemic, ridership across all modes of public transit dropped sharply and has only recovered partially in the years since, with no mode of transportation returning to 2018 baseline levels by 2025. Recovered trajectories varied substantially by transportation mode with buses rebounding earlier and more steadily, subways following with a slower, upward trend, and additional modes of public transportation lagging the longest but accelerating in recent years. These patterns likely reflect the differences in rider demographics, essential-worker travel needs, and the geographical distribution of jobs returning to in-person work. The data demonstrating that ridership has yet to return to 2018 baseline levels can likely be attributed to the advent of remote companies and the continuation of hybrid and remote work options. These factors continue to shape transit demand, even in 2025.

Our analyses also highlight that extreme weather meaningfully reduces ridership, although the severity of the effect varies by transit mode. Buses showed relatively minor declines in ridership on extreme-weather days. Subways experienced steeper drops in ridership on extreme-weather days, consistent with issues such as weather-related service disruptions and subway station flooding risks. Additional forms of public transportation–including the Long Island Railroad, Metro-North Railroad, the Staten Island Railway, and Access-A-Ride–were the most sensitive to weather impacts, likely because longer, commuter routes with their aboveground infrastructure are more vulnerable to heavy rain, snowfall, and heat-induced system failures. These findings highlight how climate variability exacerbates weaknesses in public transportation and underscore the importance of resilient infrastructure planning.

Furthermore, when considering the combined influence of weather-related events and public-health factors, our linear regression analysis shows that COVID-19 was a significant, stronger predictor of the declines in monthly ridership across all modes of transit compared to temperature. The significant negative association between COVID-19 case counts and ridership shows that COVID-19 infection risk and related transit service adjustments continued to affect transit behavior beyond the initial onset of the pandemic. On the other hand, temperature alone showed no significant association with monthly ridership. In turn, it may be beneficial to analyze the association between extreme weather days and daily ridership. That said, these results emphasize that the MTA ridership was remained highly sensitive to the COVID-19 pandemic while facing additional operational strain thanks to extreme-weather events (although insignificant).

In conclusion, post-pandemic changes in ridership behavior and increasing climate variability presents ongoing challenges for the MTA and potentially other transit agencies across the United States. Understanding which transit modes are most vulnerable–and under what conditions (both public-health- and weather-wise)–can bolster targeted investments in public transportation system resilience, rider safety, and surveillance and communication strategies. As New York City continues to adapt to this new normal, strengthening flexibility of the MTA will be vital in supporting equitable mobility, building trust in the system, and maintaining the subway as the national treasure–as Keith Gessen puts it–that it truly is.
